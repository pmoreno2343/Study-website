General poisoned patient approach

Day 25 starts with a structured approach: stabilize first, identify toxidromes, obtain targeted diagnostics, then use antidotes/decontamination selectively.

Initial stabilization

Immediate priorities

  • ABCDE first: secure airway when protective reflexes are lost; support ventilation/oxygenation; treat shock; assess neurologic status; fully expose for tox clues.
  • Check fingerstick glucose early in any altered patient and obtain a 12-lead ECG promptly when ingestion is possible.
  • Continuous cardiac monitoring, pulse oximetry, temperature, mental status, IV access and pregnancy testing when relevant.

Focused toxicology history

  • Agent(s), formulation/concentration, estimated dose, time, route, intent, co-ingestions, prescriptions/OTC/herbals, comorbidities and access to medications.
  • Use bedside patterns—pupils, skin moisture, bowel sounds, respiratory rate, temperature, mental status, neuromuscular findings—to recognize toxidromes.
  • Call poison center/medical toxicology early for severe, unknown, mixed or rapidly evolving poisoning.

Diagnostics & decontamination

  • Targeted labs commonly include electrolytes, renal/liver function, VBG/ABG, pregnancy test, acetaminophen level, salicylate level and toxin-specific testing.
  • Activated charcoal is selective: consider after a potentially toxic ingestion when benefit is plausible and the airway is protected. Do not use for caustics/hydrocarbons with high aspiration risk or obstruction/ileus.
  • Routine gastric lavage is not recommended; whole-bowel irrigation is reserved for selected sustained-release, iron, lithium or body-packer scenarios.

Disposition red flags

  • ICU/toxicology escalation for airway compromise, recurrent seizures, significant dysrhythmia/QRS-QT abnormality, shock, severe acidosis, hyperthermia, organ failure or diagnostic uncertainty.
  • Intentional overdose requires psychiatric risk assessment once medically stabilized.

My additions / weak points

Anticholinergic, cholinergic, sympathomimetic toxidromes

Differentiate three high-yield toxidromes using pupils, skin, secretions, bowel sounds, vitals and neuromuscular findings.

Pattern recognition

Anticholinergic

  • Hot, dry, flushed, mydriatic, delirious, urinary retention, decreased bowel sounds.
  • Common causes: first-generation antihistamines, TCAs, atropine/scopolamine, some antipsychotics/antispasmodics.
  • Management is largely supportive: benzodiazepines for agitation/seizures, active cooling, fluids. Physostigmine is specialist-directed and contraindicated when sodium-channel blockade/TCA toxicity is suspected.

Cholinergic

  • Muscarinic excess: salivation, lacrimation, urination, diarrhea, emesis, bronchorrhea, bradycardia, miosis.
  • Nicotinic features: fasciculations, weakness and respiratory paralysis.
  • Organophosphate/carbamate poisoning: decontaminate staff/patient appropriately, give atropine titrated to drying bronchial secretions/ventilation; pralidoxime is used early for organophosphate toxicity.

Sympathomimetic

  • Agitation, diaphoresis, mydriasis, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia; severe cases cause chest pain, seizures, rhabdomyolysis and dysrhythmias.
  • Common agents: cocaine, amphetamines, MDMA and other stimulants.
  • Benzodiazepines + active cooling are core therapy; treat complications rather than chasing the blood-pressure number in isolation.

Exam discriminator

  • Anticholinergic and sympathomimetic both may cause mydriasis/agitation; dry skin + absent bowel sounds favors anticholinergic, while diaphoresis favors sympathomimetic.

My additions / weak points

Opioid and sedative-hypnotic toxidromes

Distinguish opioid respiratory depression from sedative-hypnotic intoxication and know when naloxone or supportive ventilation is the priority.

CNS/respiratory depression

Opioid toxidrome

  • Classic triad: CNS depression + respiratory depression + miosis, but pupils may be atypical with hypoxia, co-ingestions or some synthetic opioids.
  • Support ventilation immediately; do not wait for naloxone if apneic.
  • Naloxone: titrate to adequate spontaneous ventilation rather than full arousal; repeat doses/infusion may be needed with long-acting opioids.

Sedative-hypnotic overdose

  • Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, Z-drugs and alcohol cause sedation, ataxia, slurred speech and CNS depression; severe respiratory depression often implies large dose or co-ingestion.
  • Treatment is mainly airway protection, ventilation/oxygenation, fluids and monitoring.
  • Flumazenil is not routine because it can precipitate seizures/withdrawal, especially in chronic benzodiazepine use or mixed overdose.

Co-ingestion clues

  • Mydriasis/agitation → stimulant; hyperthermia/clonus → serotonergic; dry skin/urinary retention → anticholinergic; QRS widening → sodium-channel blocker/TCA; prolonged QT → additional drug effect.

My additions / weak points

Serotonin syndrome

Recognize the rapid-onset triad and use Hunter criteria; severe hyperthermia and rigidity are life-threatening.

Neuromuscular hyperactivity

Diagnosis

  • Usually develops within hours of serotonergic exposure or interaction. Triad: mental-status change, autonomic instability, neuromuscular hyperactivity.
  • Clonus and hyperreflexia are especially important. Hunter criteria use spontaneous/inducible/ocular clonus, tremor + hyperreflexia, or hypertonia + fever + clonus.
  • Differentiate from NMS: NMS usually evolves more slowly with lead-pipe rigidity and hyporeflexia/bradyreflexia.

Management

  • Stop serotonergic agents. ABCs, IV fluids, continuous monitoring.
  • Benzodiazepines for agitation and neuromuscular excitation; external cooling for hyperthermia.
  • Cyproheptadine can be considered for persistent moderate/severe symptoms when enteral therapy is feasible.

Severe toxicity

  • Severe hyperthermia, marked rigidity, rhabdomyolysis, seizures, DIC or renal failure → ICU.
  • For extreme hyperthermia from muscle activity, rapid sedation, intubation and nondepolarizing paralysis may be required; antipyretics do not treat the mechanism.

My additions / weak points

TCA overdose: wide QRS → sodium bicarbonate

TCA toxicity combines anticholinergic effects with seizures, hypotension and life-threatening sodium-channel blockade.

Sodium-channel blockade

Recognition

  • Common findings: delirium, mydriasis, dry skin/mucosa, urinary retention, hypotension, seizures and dysrhythmias.
  • ECG: QRS ≥100 ms, terminal R in aVR, QT prolongation. QRS widening predicts seizure/ventricular arrhythmia risk.

Sodium bicarbonate

  • Give IV sodium bicarbonate for QRS widening, ventricular dysrhythmia or hypotension consistent with sodium-channel blockade.
  • Repeat boluses until QRS narrows/hemodynamics improve; alkalinization is typically continued with an infusion, often targeting serum pH around 7.50–7.55 while avoiding excessive alkalemia and monitoring potassium.

Additional management

  • Seizures: benzodiazepines. Persistent hypotension after bicarbonate/fluids: vasopressor support, commonly norepinephrine.
  • Avoid class IA/IC antiarrhythmics that worsen sodium-channel blockade. Physostigmine is contraindicated.
  • Refractory severe cardiotoxicity may prompt expert-guided lipid emulsion/ECMO consideration.

My additions / weak points

Acetaminophen overdose

Know when the nomogram applies, when it does not, and when NAC must be started before results are available.

N-acetylcysteine

Mechanism & assessment

  • NAPQI accumulation after glutathione depletion causes centrilobular hepatic necrosis.
  • For a single acute ingestion with known time, obtain acetaminophen concentration at ≥4 hours and interpret with the Rumack-Matthew nomogram.
  • Check AST/ALT, INR, creatinine and glucose; severe toxicity may evolve despite early mild symptoms.

When to start NAC

  • Start N-acetylcysteine (NAC) if the level plots at/above treatment line.
  • If presentation is >8 hours after a potentially toxic known ingestion, if the level will not be available by 8 hours, if timing is unknown with a measurable level, or if there is evidence of hepatic injury, start NAC without delay.
  • NAC is highly effective when started early but still benefits established toxicity.

Repeated supratherapeutic ingestion

  • The acute nomogram does not apply. Obtain acetaminophen level and AST/ALT regardless of clock time.
  • Treat when acetaminophen is meaningfully detectable (commonly ≥20 µg/mL) or transaminases are elevated/clinical hepatotoxicity is present, with toxicology input for complex cases.

Stopping & escalation

  • Continue NAC beyond the standard protocol if acetaminophen remains detectable, AST/ALT are rising, INR is abnormal or the patient is clinically ill.
  • Consider transplant-center consultation for rapidly worsening liver injury, encephalopathy, coagulopathy, acidosis or multiorgan failure.

My additions / weak points

Salicylate poisoning

Salicylate toxicity is dynamic: serial levels, acid-base interpretation, potassium correction and early dialysis decisions are central.

Alkalinization & dialysis

Recognition

  • Early clues: tinnitus, nausea/vomiting, diaphoresis, tachypnea, fever and agitation.
  • Classic acid-base pattern is respiratory alkalosis plus anion-gap metabolic acidosis; severe CNS/pulmonary toxicity may occur even as serum concentrations change.
  • Obtain serial salicylate levels, ABG/VBG, electrolytes, glucose and renal function.

Alkalinization

  • Correct volume depletion and hypokalemia; adequate potassium is necessary to alkalinize urine.
  • IV sodium bicarbonate is used to raise serum/urine pH and increase renal salicylate elimination. Avoid acidemia because it increases CNS penetration.

Airway danger

  • Intubation can precipitate abrupt deterioration if compensatory hyperventilation is lost. If unavoidable, minimize apnea and maintain very high minute ventilation to match pre-intubation respiratory alkalosis while correcting acidemia.

Hemodialysis

  • Consult early for altered mental status, pulmonary edema, severe acidemia, renal failure, rising levels despite therapy, inability to alkalinize, or severe clinical toxicity. Clinical status matters more than one level.

My additions / weak points

Carbon monoxide

CO poisoning is a tissue-hypoxia syndrome; conventional pulse oximetry may look normal.

Co-oximetry & oxygen

Recognition & diagnosis

  • Think of CO with headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, chest pain, confusion, syncope or multiple people ill after the same combustion exposure.
  • Confirm with co-oximetry/carboxyhemoglobin; conventional two-wavelength pulse oximetry is unreliable.
  • COHb level supports diagnosis but correlates poorly with clinical severity; timing since exposure matters.

Management

  • Give 100% oxygen promptly, generally until symptoms resolve, with serial neurologic reassessment.
  • In moderate/severe poisoning, obtain ECG/troponin because myocardial injury increases long-term risk.

Hyperbaric oxygen considerations

  • Consider HBO for severe poisoning such as COHb >25–30%, cardiac involvement, severe acidosis, loss of consciousness, neurologic impairment or abnormal neuropsychologic testing.
  • Pregnancy warrants a lower threshold because fetal hemoglobin binds CO avidly and fetal clearance is slower.

Follow-up

  • Warn about delayed neurocognitive sequelae—memory, mood, gait and movement problems may appear days to weeks later. Arrange follow-up after significant poisoning.

My additions / weak points

Methanol / ethylene glycol

Both toxic alcohols produce high-anion-gap acidosis; methanol targets the optic system, ethylene glycol the kidney.

Toxic alcohol emergency

Diagnostic clues

  • Early after ingestion the osmolar gap may be elevated; as parent alcohol is metabolized, osmolar gap can fall while anion-gap metabolic acidosis worsens.
  • Methanol: blurred/snowfield vision, optic toxicity, CNS/basal-ganglia injury.
  • Ethylene glycol: hypocalcemia, calcium oxalate crystals, flank pain and AKI.

Immediate treatment

  • If significant toxic alcohol exposure is suspected, obtain ABG/VBG, electrolytes, anion/osmolar gaps, renal function and urinalysis—but do not delay fomepizole when suspicion is high.
  • Correct severe acidosis with sodium bicarbonate. Give folate/folinic acid for methanol; thiamine + pyridoxine are used as adjunct cofactors for ethylene glycol.

Dialysis triggers

  • Hemodialysis is used for severe acidosis, visual deficits (methanol), significant renal injury (ethylene glycol), coma/seizures, clinical deterioration, or sufficiently high measured toxic-alcohol levels.
  • Continue ADH blockade during dialysis. Intermittent hemodialysis is preferred when available for severe methanol poisoning.

My additions / weak points

Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)

LAST is a biphasic CNS→cardiovascular toxicity where early recognition and lipid emulsion can be lifesaving.

Lipid rescue

Recognition

  • Early neurologic signs: circumoral numbness, metallic taste, tinnitus, agitation, visual/auditory disturbance, tremor or seizures.
  • Cardiovascular toxicity: conduction delay/QRS widening, ventricular dysrhythmias, hypotension and cardiovascular collapse.
  • Can occur after inadvertent intravascular injection, excessive dose or rapid systemic absorption.

Immediate actions

  • Stop local anesthetic, call for help, give 100% oxygen and avoid hypoxia/acidosis.
  • Seizures: benzodiazepines preferred; avoid large propofol doses if unstable.
  • Start 20% lipid emulsion early for significant CNS or cardiovascular toxicity.

Lipid emulsion & modified ACLS

  • ASRA teaching for patients <70 kg: bolus ~1.5 mL/kg over 2–3 min, then infuse ~0.25 mL/kg/min; repeat bolus and double infusion if unstable. Continue after hemodynamic stability per checklist.
  • Use small epinephrine doses if needed; avoid vasopressin, calcium-channel blockers, beta-blockers and additional sodium-channel blockers such as lidocaine/procainamide.

Refractory collapse

  • Persistent cardiovascular collapse despite lipid + modified ACLS → consider cardiopulmonary bypass/ECMO early where available.
