1. Tachyarrhythmia Approach

Stability first, then QRS width and rhythm regularity.

Arrhythmia

Definition & first question

A tachyarrhythmia is an abnormally rapid rhythm, usually clinically important when the ventricular rate is around ≥150/min. The immediate question is not the rhythm name—it is whether the patient is unstable because of the tachycardia.

🔥 Unstable = synchronized cardioversion

  • Hypotension or shock
  • Acute altered mental status
  • Ischemic chest discomfort
  • Acute heart failure / pulmonary edema

Sedate when feasible, but do not delay cardioversion in a crashing patient. If the patient has no pulse, use the cardiac-arrest pathway rather than synchronized cardioversion.

Narrow-complex tachycardia (<120 ms)

  • Regular: sinus tachycardia, AVNRT, AVRT, atrial tachycardia. Treat physiologic sinus tachycardia by correcting the cause. For regular SVT, vagal maneuvers then adenosine when appropriate.
  • Irregular: atrial fibrillation, flutter with variable block, multifocal atrial tachycardia.

💊 Adenosine

Initial6 mg rapid IV push + flush
If needed12 mg rapid IV push

Use only for appropriate regular rhythms. Avoid reflexive use in irregular wide-complex tachycardia or pre-excited AF.

Wide-complex tachycardia (≥120 ms)

In an adult with structural heart disease or an uncertain diagnosis, a regular wide-complex tachycardia should be treated as ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise.

  • VT clues: AV dissociation, capture/fusion beats, concordance, prior MI/cardiomyopathy.
  • Stable monomorphic wide-complex tachycardia may require an antiarrhythmic infusion and expert consultation.
  • Adenosine can be considered only if the rhythm is regular and monomorphic.

Dangerous irregular wide-complex rhythms

  • AF with aberrancy
  • Polymorphic VT / torsades de pointes
  • Pre-excited AF (WPW)

In pre-excited AF, avoid AV-nodal blockers such as beta-blockers, diltiazem/verapamil, digoxin and adenosine because they may accelerate conduction over the accessory pathway.

Torsades de pointes

Polymorphic VT with a prolonged QT: stop QT-prolonging drugs, correct K/Mg, and give IV magnesium. Unstable or pulseless polymorphic VT requires immediate defibrillation.

Memory line

Fast rhythm → stable or unstable → narrow or wide → regular or irregular.

2. Atrial Fibrillation & Flutter

Rate/rhythm strategy plus stroke prevention.

High yield

Classic presentation & ECG

  • AF: irregularly irregular ventricular rhythm, absent organized P waves.
  • Atrial flutter: organized atrial activity, often saw-tooth waves; ventricular response may be regular or variable.
  • Symptoms range from none to palpitations, dyspnea, fatigue, chest discomfort, syncope or decompensated HF.

Unstable AF/flutter

Hypotension, shock, ischemia or acute pulmonary edema attributable to the rhythm → immediate synchronized cardioversion.

Stable patient: rate vs rhythm

  • Rate control: beta-blocker or diltiazem/verapamil when appropriate. Digoxin may be useful in selected patients, especially when BP is low or HF is present.
  • Rhythm control: increasingly considered early in appropriate symptomatic patients; options include cardioversion, antiarrhythmic therapy and catheter ablation.
  • Treat triggers: infection, thyroid disease, alcohol, electrolyte disturbance, ACS, PE, HF.

Stroke prevention

The 2024 ESC guideline uses CHA₂DS₂-VA (without sex category) for thromboembolic risk; many systems still use CHA₂DS₂-VASc. Follow the locally adopted framework.

  • DOACs are preferred for many eligible patients with AF.
  • Warfarin remains standard for mechanical prosthetic valves and moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis.
  • Bleeding scores help identify modifiable risk but should not be used alone to deny indicated anticoagulation.

Cardioversion & anticoagulation nuance

  • If AF duration is ≥48 h or unknown, elective cardioversion generally requires therapeutic anticoagulation before/after cardioversion or a TEE-guided strategy.
  • Hemodynamic instability overrides the elective timing rule: cardiovert immediately and address anticoagulation as soon as feasible.

WPW trap

Irregular wide-complex tachycardia with pre-excitation → avoid AV-nodal blockers. Use an expert/emergency pathway and consider procainamide or cardioversion depending on stability.

Memory line

AF management = AF-CARE: comorbidities, avoid stroke, rate/rhythm control, evaluate/reassess.

3. Bradycardia & AV Block

Treat the patient, identify the block, anticipate pacing.

ACLS

When is bradycardia clinically important?

Bradyarrhythmia is usually considered clinically significant at HR <50/min when it causes hypotension, acute altered mental status, shock, ischemic discomfort or acute HF.

🔥 Symptomatic / unstable pathway

Atropine1 mg IV every 3–5 min; max 3 mg

If ineffective: transcutaneous pacing and/or dopamine 5–20 mcg/kg/min or epinephrine 2–10 mcg/min. Consider transvenous pacing and urgent expert consultation.

Reversible causes

  • Myocardial ischemia/infarction
  • Beta-blocker, calcium-channel blocker or digoxin toxicity
  • Hyperkalemia and other electrolyte abnormalities
  • Hypoxia, hypothermia, hypothyroidism, increased vagal tone

AV-block recognition

BlockECG clueClinical pearl
1st degreePR >200 ms; every P conductsUsually benign
Mobitz IProgressive PR lengthening → dropped QRSUsually AV-node level
Mobitz IIConstant PR with sudden dropped QRSInfranodal; pacing mindset
3rd degreeP waves and QRS dissociatedOften requires pacing

Higher-level traps

  • Atropine may be ineffective in high-grade infranodal block; do not delay pacing when the patient is unstable.
  • Inferior MI can cause AV-nodal block; anterior MI with new high-grade block is more ominous and often infranodal.
  • A patient can have a “normal” BP yet still have poor perfusion from bradycardia.

Memory line

Mobitz II + complete heart block = urgent pacing mindset.

4. Heart Failure: Classification & Diagnosis

Phenotype, congestion, natriuretic peptides and echocardiography.

Heart failure

Definition & EF phenotypes

  • HFrEF: LVEF ≤40%
  • HFmrEF: LVEF 41–49%
  • HFpEF: LVEF ≥50% with evidence supporting elevated filling pressures/structural or functional cardiac abnormality

HF is a clinical syndrome; EF alone does not diagnose HF.

Classic presentation

  • Dyspnea, orthopnea, PND, exercise intolerance
  • Peripheral edema, weight gain, elevated JVP
  • Crackles, S3, hepatojugular reflux
  • Right-sided HF: systemic venous congestion, hepatomegaly/ascites, edema

BNP / NT-proBNP

Elevated natriuretic peptides support HF in the correct context; low values make HF less likely. Values can be higher with age, renal dysfunction and AF, and lower in obesity.

Core diagnostic tests

  • ECG: rhythm, prior infarct, LVH, conduction disease.
  • CXR: congestion, edema, pleural effusions, alternative lung disease.
  • Echo: EF, chamber size, diastolic function, valves, RV function and pulmonary pressures.
  • Labs: CBC, electrolytes, renal/liver function, glucose/HbA1c, thyroid testing, iron studies when appropriate.

Common precipitants of decompensation

  • ACS or ischemia
  • Arrhythmia, especially AF with RVR
  • Uncontrolled hypertension
  • Infection
  • Medication/diet nonadherence
  • Renal dysfunction, anemia, thyroid disease, drug toxicity

Red flags

Shock, severe hypoxemia, rapidly worsening pulmonary edema, new significant murmur, syncope, rising lactate/creatinine or oliguria require urgent escalation.

5. Acute Decompensated HF / Pulmonary Edema

Stabilize, phenotype, decongest and treat the precipitant.

Emergency HF

Immediate assessment

  • ABCDE, BP, SpO₂, ECG, monitor, IV access
  • Identify pulmonary edema and signs of shock
  • ECG, troponin when ischemia possible, renal function/electrolytes, BNP/NT-proBNP, CXR; bedside echo/POCUS when useful

Respiratory support

  • Give oxygen for hypoxemia—not routinely to a normoxic patient.
  • Non-invasive ventilation (CPAP/BiPAP) can reduce work of breathing and improve oxygenation in significant pulmonary edema when appropriate.
  • Escalate airway support if mental status, fatigue or gas exchange worsens.

Decongestion

IV loop diuretic is standard for congestion. Initial dose depends on prior loop-diuretic exposure, renal function and severity. Assess urine output, symptoms, weight, renal function and electrolytes; intensify the diuretic strategy if response is inadequate.

Cardiogenic shock

Hypotension plus poor perfusion/end-organ dysfunction requires ICU-level management, urgent cause identification and often vasoactive support. Norepinephrine is commonly favored when vasopressor support is needed; an inotrope such as dobutamine may be considered for persistent low-output physiology.

Hypertensive pulmonary edema

Marked hypertension with acute pulmonary edema may respond rapidly to non-invasive ventilation plus vasodilator therapy such as nitrates when BP allows, while congestion and the precipitating cause are addressed.

Do not miss the precipitant

  • ACS
  • AF with RVR or other arrhythmia
  • Acute MR/mechanical complication
  • Hypertensive crisis
  • Infection
  • Medication nonadherence / renal failure

Trap

A patient with wet lungs and cardiogenic shock is not treated with indiscriminate large crystalloid boluses. Reassess hemodynamics and congestion repeatedly.

6. Chronic HFrEF Guideline-Directed Therapy

The four pillars, diuretics, devices and follow-up.

GDMT

🔥 The four pillars of HFrEF

  1. ARNI (preferred when appropriate) or ACEi/ARB
  2. Evidence-based beta-blocker: carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, or bisoprolol
  3. MRA: spironolactone or eplerenone when renal function/K permit
  4. SGLT2 inhibitor: dapagliflozin or empagliflozin, regardless of diabetes status

Start foundational therapies early at tolerated doses rather than waiting to maximize one drug before adding the next.

Diuretics & congestion

Loop diuretics improve symptoms and congestion but are not one of the four mortality-reducing foundational drug classes. Adjust dose to volume status, BP, renal function and electrolytes.

Selected add-ons

  • Hydralazine/isosorbide dinitrate in selected patients or when RAAS-inhibiting therapy is not tolerated.
  • Ivabradine in selected symptomatic patients with LVEF ≤35%, sinus rhythm and HR ≥70 despite maximally tolerated beta-blocker.
  • Digoxin may reduce hospitalizations in selected symptomatic patients but requires toxicity awareness.

Monitoring after starting/titrating GDMT

  • BP, orthostasis, heart rate
  • Creatinine/eGFR and potassium
  • Volume status and daily weight
  • Adherence, sodium intake and precipitating factors

Device basics

  • ICD: primary prevention in selected patients with persistently reduced EF despite optimized therapy and adequate expected survival.
  • CRT: strongest benefit in selected symptomatic patients with reduced EF and wide QRS, especially LBBB with QRS ≥150 ms.

Medication traps

  • Do not combine ACEi with ARB routinely.
  • Allow an appropriate washout when switching between ACEi and sacubitril/valsartan to reduce angioedema risk.
  • NSAIDs can worsen fluid retention and renal function.
  • Non-DHP CCBs such as verapamil/diltiazem are generally avoided in HFrEF because of negative inotropy.

Memory line

HFrEF = 4 pillars early + diuretic for congestion + devices when criteria are met.

7. HFmrEF, HFpEF & Right-Sided HF

Preserved EF does not mean normal heart.

HF phenotypes

HFpEF diagnosis

HF symptoms/signs with LVEF ≥50% plus objective evidence of elevated filling pressures or structural/functional cardiac abnormalities. Common associations include older age, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, CKD, AF and sleep apnea.

HFpEF / HFmrEF management

  • SGLT2 inhibitors reduce HF hospitalization/cardiovascular events across mildly reduced and preserved EF populations and are a major contemporary therapy.
  • Diuretics treat congestion.
  • Control BP and manage AF, ischemia, obesity, diabetes, CKD and sleep apnea.
  • Selected patients may benefit from MRA/RAAS-directed therapy depending on phenotype, EF range and guideline context.

Natriuretic-peptide nuance

Obesity can suppress BNP/NT-proBNP; AF and CKD can increase levels. Interpret the value in context rather than using one cutoff in isolation.

Right-sided HF

  • Elevated JVP, peripheral edema, hepatomegaly, ascites, weight gain
  • Causes: left HF, pulmonary hypertension, RV infarction, chronic lung disease, TR, congenital disease
  • Echo evaluates RV size/function, TR and pulmonary pressures.
  • Diuresis treats systemic congestion, but overly aggressive preload reduction can be harmful in selected RV-failure states.

RV infarction pearl

Inferior MI + hypotension + elevated JVP + relatively clear lungs → think RV infarction. Obtain right-sided leads. Avoid nitrates if preload-dependent hypotension is present.

Memory line

HFpEF: diagnose the syndrome + prove filling-pressure/structural abnormality + treat congestion and comorbidities.

8. Valvular Heart Disease & Murmurs

Recognize the murmur, predict the radiation, know the emergency clues.

Murmurs

Aortic stenosis

  • Crescendo-decrescendo systolic ejection murmur at RUSB → carotids
  • Severe classic symptoms: angina, syncope, dyspnea/HF
  • Echo defines severity; symptomatic severe AS requires valve-team evaluation for intervention.

Mitral regurgitation

  • Holosystolic blowing murmur at apex → axilla
  • Chronic: degenerative disease, ischemic/functional MR, rheumatic disease
  • Acute severe MR after MI can cause abrupt pulmonary edema and shock and may produce a surprisingly soft murmur.

Mitral stenosis

  • Opening snap + low-pitched diastolic rumble at apex
  • Usually rheumatic globally; causes LA enlargement, AF and pulmonary hypertension
  • AF with rheumatic moderate-severe MS → warfarin rather than a DOAC.

Aortic regurgitation

  • Early diastolic decrescendo murmur, usually LSB
  • Chronic severe AR may cause bounding pulses and wide pulse pressure
  • Acute severe AR can cause pulmonary edema/shock and is an emergency, especially with endocarditis or aortic dissection.

MVP & tricuspid regurgitation

  • MVP: mid-systolic click ± late systolic murmur; click/murmur occurs earlier with standing/Valsalva.
  • TR: holosystolic murmur at LLSB that increases with inspiration (Carvallo sign).

Dynamic-maneuver pearl

  • Valsalva/standing decreases preload: most murmurs become softer, but HCM becomes louder and MVP occurs earlier.
  • Squatting/leg raise increases preload: HCM softens; many flow murmurs become louder.

Urgent red flags

Syncope with severe AS, new murmur with pulmonary edema/shock, fever with new regurgitant murmur/embolic signs, or suspected acute AR/MR require urgent evaluation.
