📅 Day 1

ABCDE Approach

Systematic first assessment of the unstable patient.

Foundation

Core rule

Treat immediately life-threatening abnormalities as you find them. Do not wait to finish the entire survey.

A — Airway

  • Can the patient speak?
  • Stridor, gurgling, snoring, foreign body, facial burns, hoarseness.
  • Trauma: protect C-spine.

B — Breathing

  • RR, SpO₂, chest expansion, breath sounds, work of breathing.
  • Tension pneumothorax: clinical diagnosis → immediate decompression.

C — Circulation

  • Pulse, BP, cap refill, skin, JVP, bleeding, ECG.
  • Control hemorrhage; obtain IV/IO access.
  • Normal BP does not exclude early shock.

D / E

  • D: GCS, pupils, focal signs, seizures, bedside glucose.
  • E: expose fully while preventing hypothermia.

Memory line

Airway → Breathing → Circulation → Brain → Body

Common traps

  • CT before glucose in AMS.
  • X-ray before treating tension pneumothorax.
  • Ignoring C-spine protection.

Shock

Poor tissue perfusion; recognize the phenotype.

Very high yield
TypeClassic cluesJVP/lungsFirst priorities
HypovolemicBleeding/fluid loss, cool skin, tachycardiaLow JVP, usually clear lungsControl source + fluids/blood
CardiogenicMI/HF, cool skinHigh JVP, pulmonary edemaTreat cause; cautious fluids; vasoactive support as indicated
DistributiveSepsis/anaphylaxis; may be warm earlyVariableFluids + treat cause ± vasopressor
ObstructivePE, tension PTX, tamponadeOften raised JVPRelieve obstruction urgently

Poor perfusion clues

  • Altered mental status
  • Oliguria
  • Cool/mottled skin (not always in early distributive shock)
  • Tachycardia
  • Elevated lactate may support impaired perfusion.

Pattern shortcuts

Dry + flat neck veins → hypovolemic.

Wet lungs + raised JVP → cardiogenic.

Warm + infection → septic.

Trap

Do not give indiscriminate large fluid boluses in cardiogenic shock.

Sepsis & Septic Shock

Infection with organ dysfunction; resuscitate early.

Emergency

Recognition

  • Suspected/confirmed infection + acute organ dysfunction.
  • Clues: hypotension, tachypnea, altered mentation, oliguria, AKI, hypoxemia, thrombocytopenia, elevated lactate.

Initial treatment

  • Obtain cultures if this does not cause meaningful delay.
  • Give appropriate antimicrobials promptly in septic shock/high-likelihood sepsis.
  • Crystalloids are first-line; reassess response dynamically.
  • Source control is essential.

Vasopressors

  • Norepinephrine first line.
  • Typical initial MAP target: 65 mmHg.

Must memorize

Sepsis + hypoperfusion → fluids + antibiotics + source control.

Persistent hypotension → norepinephrine.

Traps

  • Waiting for cultures before treating shock.
  • Endless fluids without reassessment.
  • Forgetting source control.

Anaphylaxis

Epinephrine first.

Must memorize

Classic presentation

Rapid systemic allergic reaction with airway/breathing/circulatory compromise and/or skin-mucosal findings after likely allergen exposure.

First-line treatment

IM epinephrine in mid-anterolateral thigh.

0.01 mg/kg of 1 mg/mL, max 0.5 mg adult

May repeat every 5–15 min if needed.

Adjuncts

  • Airway/oxygen as needed.
  • IV fluids for hypotension.
  • Bronchodilator for persistent wheeze.
  • Antihistamines/steroids are adjuncts only.

Memory

Anaphylaxis → IM epinephrine first.

Traps

  • Antihistamine first.
  • Waiting for hypotension before epinephrine.

Altered Mental Status / Hypoglycemia

Fix reversible threats first.

Common vignette

Immediate approach

  • ABCDE.
  • Bedside glucose early.
  • Check oxygenation, temperature, medications/toxins, trauma, infection, focal neurologic signs.

Hypoglycemia treatment

  • Conscious and able to swallow → rapid oral carbohydrate.
  • Severe/unconscious → IV dextrose if access.
  • No IV access → glucagon.
  • Recheck glucose and identify cause.

Memory

Confused patient → glucose early.

Trap

CT brain before checking glucose in an unstable/confused patient.

Heat Illness

CNS dysfunction is the key separator.

Pattern recognition
Heat exhaustionHeat stroke
CNSNo severe CNS dysfunctionAltered mental status, seizure, coma
TemperatureElevatedMarked hyperthermia
TreatmentCooling + fluids as appropriateRapid active cooling + resuscitation

Complications

  • Rhabdomyolysis
  • AKI
  • Hepatic injury
  • Coagulopathy
  • Electrolyte abnormalities

Memory

Heat stroke = hyperthermia + CNS dysfunction.

Trap

Do not require dry skin; sweating may still be present.

Burns

Airway, depth, TBSA, fluids, complications.

High yield

Initial approach

  • Stop the burning process; remove constricting items.
  • ABCDE.
  • Assess for inhalation injury: facial burns, soot, hoarseness, stridor, enclosed-space fire.
  • Estimate burn size using TBSA.

Fluids

Large burns may require formal fluid resuscitation. Classic Parkland formula:

4 mL × kg × %TBSA of lactated Ringer’s in first 24 h; half in first 8 h from time of burn, remainder over next 16 h.

Use urine output and clinical response to guide ongoing resuscitation.

Do not count

Superficial/first-degree burns are generally not included in TBSA calculations for resuscitation.

Red flags

  • Inhalation injury / airway edema
  • Circumferential burns
  • Electrical or chemical burns
  • Major burns of face, hands, genitalia, joints

Memory

Facial burn + hoarseness/soot → think inhalation injury → early airway planning.

Trauma Basics

Primary survey first; secondary survey after stabilization.

ABCDE + C-spine

Primary survey

  • A: airway + C-spine protection
  • B: breathing/chest life threats
  • C: circulation + hemorrhage control
  • D: neurologic status/GCS
  • E: exposure while preventing hypothermia

Hemorrhage

Catastrophic external bleeding must be controlled immediately. In major hemorrhage, prioritize blood-product-based resuscitation and definitive bleeding control rather than large volumes of crystalloid.

Secondary survey

Head-to-toe examination, history, and detailed injury assessment after immediate life threats have been addressed.

Memory

Primary survey saves life; secondary survey finds the rest.

Traps

  • Detailed history before treating life threats.
  • Missing C-spine protection.
  • Failing to expose the patient.
