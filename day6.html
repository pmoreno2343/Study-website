1. Thyroid Axis & TSH / Free T4 Patterns

Read the pattern first, then ask whether the problem is thyroidal, pituitary, medication-related or illness-related.

TFT interpretation

Normal physiology

Hypothalamic TRH stimulates pituitary TSH, which stimulates thyroid T4/T3 production. Circulating thyroid hormone suppresses TRH and TSH by negative feedback.

Core patterns

TSHFree T4Think
↑↓Primary hypothyroidism
↑NormalSubclinical hypothyroidism
↓↑Primary hyperthyroidism
↓NormalSubclinical hyperthyroidism or T3 toxicosis
Low/normal↓Central hypothyroidism

T3 toxicosis

Suppressed TSH + normal FT4 does not exclude hyperthyroidism. Check T3 if clinical suspicion is high.

Non-thyroidal illness

Acute/severe systemic illness can produce low T3, sometimes low FT4, with variable TSH. Avoid diagnosing chronic thyroid disease from one abnormal panel during critical illness unless the clinical picture strongly supports it.

Biotin interference

High-dose biotin can cause falsely low TSH and falsely high FT4/T3 in many immunoassays. Ask about supplements when labs and clinical findings do not match.

Memory line

Primary thyroid disease: TSH moves opposite FT4. Central disease: FT4 is low but TSH fails to rise appropriately.

Pregnancy

hCG can physiologically lower TSH, especially in the first trimester. Use pregnancy- and trimester-specific reference ranges when available.

2. Hypothyroidism & Levothyroxine

Differentiate overt, subclinical and central hypothyroidism and know how to start and monitor replacement.

Levothyroxine

Classic presentation

Fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, bradycardia, delayed reflex relaxation, menstrual change and cognitive slowing. Hashimoto disease is a common cause in iodine-sufficient settings.

Diagnosis

  • Primary overt: high TSH + low FT4.
  • Subclinical: high TSH + normal FT4.
  • Central: low/inappropriately normal TSH + low FT4.

Subclinical hypothyroidism

Treatment is most strongly favored with persistent TSH ≥10 mIU/L, pregnancy/trying to conceive, or selected symptomatic/high-risk patients. Individualize milder TSH elevation.

Levothyroxine dosing

Young healthy adults needing full replacement often require about 1.6 mcg/kg/day. Start lower and titrate slowly in older adults or known coronary disease.

Monitoring

Recheck TSH about 6–8 weeks after starting or changing levothyroxine. In central hypothyroidism, follow FT4 rather than TSH.

Administration

Take consistently on an empty stomach. Separate from iron, calcium and certain binding agents/antacids by several hours because absorption can fall.

Critical trap

If central hypothyroidism is possible, evaluate adrenal function before giving thyroid hormone when adrenal insufficiency is a concern. Thyroid replacement can precipitate adrenal crisis in cortisol-deficient patients.

3. Hyperthyroidism: Graves, Toxic Nodules & Thyroiditis

Confirm thyrotoxicosis, identify the mechanism and choose therapy based on the cause.

Thyrotoxicosis

Biochemical pattern

Suppressed TSH with elevated FT4 and/or T3. If FT4 is normal, check T3 when T3 toxicosis is suspected.

Graves disease

Diffuse goiter, ophthalmopathy, dermopathy and positive TRAb/TSI support Graves. TRAb positivity can establish the diagnosis without uptake scanning in many patients.

Toxic nodule / toxic multinodular goiter

Autonomous hormone production gives focal or patchy increased uptake. Toxic nodules generally do not remit with antithyroid drugs; radioactive iodine or surgery is often definitive.

Thyroiditis

Painful subacute or painless/postpartum thyroiditis releases preformed hormone, so uptake is low and antithyroid drugs are usually not useful. Treat symptoms; NSAIDs ± glucocorticoid for significant subacute pain.

Graves treatment options

Beta-blocker for symptoms. Methimazole is usually preferred over PTU except selected situations such as first-trimester pregnancy or thyroid storm. Definitive options include radioactive iodine or thyroidectomy.

Agranulocytosis warning

Fever or sore throat while taking methimazole/PTU → stop the drug and obtain an urgent CBC. Hepatotoxicity is another important adverse effect, especially with PTU.

RAI contraindication

Do not use radioactive iodine during pregnancy or breastfeeding. It may worsen active Graves orbitopathy in susceptible patients.

4. Thyroid Storm & Myxedema Coma

Recognize endocrine decompensation clinically and treat before laboratory confirmation.

Emergency

Thyroid storm

Severe thyrotoxicosis with fever, marked tachycardia/AF, agitation/delirium, GI-hepatic dysfunction and/or heart failure. Common triggers include infection, surgery, trauma, iodine exposure and medication nonadherence.

Treatment sequence

  1. ICU/supportive care + treat trigger
  2. Beta-blocker when appropriate
  3. Thionamide
  4. Iodine after thionamide
  5. Glucocorticoid

Do not over-trust hormone levels

Thyroid storm is a clinical diagnosis. Hormone concentrations may overlap those of uncomplicated thyrotoxicosis.

Myxedema coma

Severe hypothyroid decompensation: altered mentation, hypothermia, bradycardia, hypotension, hypoventilation, hyponatremia and hypoglycemia.

Treatment principles

ICU support, IV levothyroxine, empiric hydrocortisone until adrenal insufficiency is excluded, ventilatory/hemodynamic support, gentle rewarming and correction of glucose/electrolytes.

Memory line

Storm = hot, fast, agitated. Myxedema = cold, slow, hypoventilating.

5. Thyroid Nodule Diagnostic Algorithm

TSH determines whether scintigraphy is needed; ultrasound risk determines whether FNA is needed.

Nodule

First steps

History/exam for malignancy risk, obtain TSH and perform diagnostic thyroid/neck ultrasound for clinically relevant nodules.

If TSH is suppressed

Obtain a radionuclide uptake scan. A clearly hyperfunctioning (“hot”) nodule is rarely malignant and usually does not require FNA; treat the hyperthyroidism as indicated.

If TSH is normal or high

Use ultrasound risk pattern plus nodule size to decide FNA. Suspicious features include solid hypoechoic composition, irregular margins, microcalcifications/punctate echogenic foci, taller-than-wide shape, extrathyroidal extension and abnormal cervical nodes.

Clinical red flags

  • Childhood neck irradiation
  • Rapid growth
  • Hard/fixed nodule
  • Hoarseness or compressive symptoms
  • Suspicious cervical lymphadenopathy
  • Family history of thyroid cancer/MEN2

Bethesda concept

FNA cytology is reported in Bethesda categories. Benign cytology usually receives surveillance; suspicious/malignant categories often require surgery; indeterminate categories may need repeat FNA, molecular testing or diagnostic surgery depending on context.

Cancer pearls

Papillary thyroid carcinoma is the most common and tends to spread lymphatically. Follicular carcinoma spreads hematogenously. Medullary carcinoma arises from C cells, produces calcitonin and may be associated with MEN2. Anaplastic carcinoma is rare and highly aggressive.

6. Primary vs Secondary Adrenal Insufficiency

Use ACTH, potassium and pigmentation to localize cortisol deficiency.

Adrenal

Shared symptoms

Fatigue, weakness, weight loss, nausea, abdominal discomfort, hypotension/orthostasis and hyponatremia. Hypoglycemia can occur, especially in severe disease.

Primary adrenal insufficiency

  • Low cortisol + high ACTH
  • Aldosterone deficiency → hyperkalemia, salt craving, volume depletion
  • Hyperpigmentation from high ACTH/POMC activity
  • Autoimmune adrenalitis is a major cause

Secondary/tertiary adrenal insufficiency

  • Low cortisol + low/inappropriately normal ACTH
  • Aldosterone generally preserved → hyperkalemia usually absent
  • No hyperpigmentation
  • Think pituitary/hypothalamic disease or chronic glucocorticoid suppression

Testing

Morning cortisol + ACTH can screen. Standard 250-mcg cosyntropin stimulation testing is commonly used to confirm adrenal insufficiency when clinically stable. Interpret cortisol thresholds according to the assay.

Primary AI additional testing

Measure renin and aldosterone to assess mineralocorticoid deficiency, then investigate etiology such as autoimmune disease, infection, hemorrhage or infiltrative disease.

Chronic treatment

Physiologic glucocorticoid replacement is required. Primary AI with aldosterone deficiency also needs fludrocortisone. Education and stress dosing are essential.

Trap

Normal potassium does not exclude adrenal insufficiency—particularly secondary disease.

7. Addisonian Crisis & Sick-Day Rules

Unexplained shock plus GI/electrolyte clues: treat first, confirm later.

Emergency steroids

Suspect adrenal crisis when

Hypotension/shock, vomiting/abdominal pain, severe weakness, dehydration, confusion, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia or hypoglycemia occurs in known/suspected adrenal insufficiency or after steroid withdrawal.

Immediate treatment

Hydrocortisone100 mg IV/IM immediately
Then200 mg/24 h continuous or divided q6h

Give isotonic crystalloid; add dextrose if hypoglycemic. Treat the precipitating illness.

Do not wait for labs

If feasible, draw cortisol/ACTH before treatment, but never delay hydrocortisone in suspected crisis.

Sick-day rules

Increase glucocorticoid dosing during febrile illness or significant physiologic stress according to the patient’s individualized plan. If vomiting prevents oral medication, use emergency parenteral glucocorticoid and seek urgent care.

Prevention kit

  • Steroid emergency card/medical alert identification
  • Emergency hydrocortisone injection kit
  • Patient and family training
  • Perioperative stress-dose plan

Exam pearl

Refractory hypotension that improves after glucocorticoid administration is an important clue to adrenal crisis.

8. Pituitary Adenomas: Prolactinoma, Acromegaly & Cushing Disease

Recognize hormone excess syndromes and know the first diagnostic test and first-line treatment.

Pituitary

Prolactinoma

Women: galactorrhea, amenorrhea/oligomenorrhea, infertility. Men: low libido, erectile dysfunction, infertility; macroadenomas are more common at diagnosis. Check prolactin and exclude pregnancy, hypothyroidism, renal disease and dopamine-antagonist drugs.

Prolactinoma treatment

Cabergoline is generally preferred dopamine-agonist therapy because it is highly effective and often shrinks tumors. Surgery is reserved for selected resistant/intolerant or compressive cases.

Acromegaly

Enlarged hands/feet, coarse facial features, prognathism, sweating, headaches, OSA, hypertension, diabetes, arthropathy and cardiomyopathy. Screen with IGF-1; confirm biochemically and obtain pituitary MRI after biochemical evidence.

Acromegaly treatment

Transsphenoidal surgery is typically first-line for resectable pituitary disease. Persistent disease may require somatostatin receptor ligands, pegvisomant, cabergoline and/or radiotherapy depending on the case.

Cushing disease

Pituitary ACTH adenoma causing endogenous hypercortisolism. Do not jump straight to pituitary MRI. First establish Cushing syndrome biochemically using an appropriate screening test such as late-night salivary cortisol, 24-h urinary free cortisol or 1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression.

Cushing disease treatment

Once ACTH-dependent pituitary disease is established, transsphenoidal selective adenomectomy by an experienced pituitary surgeon is first-line.

Mass-effect red flags

Headache, visual-field loss (classically bitemporal hemianopia), ophthalmoplegia or multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies require urgent structural evaluation.

9. Pituitary Apoplexy & Incidentalomas

Apoplexy is an emergency; incidentalomas require structured endocrine and visual assessment.

Pituitary emergency

🔥 Pituitary apoplexy

Acute hemorrhage/infarction into a pituitary tumor can present with sudden severe headache, vomiting, visual loss/field deficit, ophthalmoplegia, altered consciousness and acute adrenal insufficiency.

Immediate management

Stabilize ABCs, check electrolytes/glucose/cortisol and pituitary hormones, obtain urgent MRI if available, give stress-dose hydrocortisone promptly when adrenal insufficiency is suspected, and involve endocrinology/neurosurgery urgently.

When surgery becomes urgent

Severe or worsening visual deficits, reduced consciousness or significant neurologic deterioration favor urgent neurosurgical decompression.

Pituitary incidentaloma: initial evaluation

  • History/exam for hormone excess and deficiency
  • Laboratory screening for hypersecretion and hypopituitarism
  • Dedicated pituitary MRI when needed
  • Formal visual fields if the lesion abuts/compresses the optic chiasm

Follow-up concept

Lesions not meeting surgical criteria receive serial imaging and endocrine follow-up. Macroincidentalomas are generally imaged sooner than microincidentalomas.

Surgical referral indications

Visual-field deficit, other compression-related neurologic/ocular deficits, lesion abutting the optic nerves/chiasm, apoplexy with visual disturbance, or a hypersecreting tumor other than a prolactinoma.

Trap

Do not assume every incidental pituitary lesion is nonfunctioning. Evaluate both hormone excess and hormone deficiency.
